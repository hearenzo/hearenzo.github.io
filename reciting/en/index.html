The Reciting Machine

읊는 기계

Listen to the two recordingsMaking the archive

In a film from 1969, Seoul Station still exists in the future tense. The trains moved to the new station in 2004. Yet when the voice plays again upstairs in the old station’s Small Dining Room, Seoul Station is still about to become something.

I collected Korean Newsreels and cultural films to find out what the old station sounded like. Two thirds of the sound was narration. You can scarcely hear what people might have said to each other on the platform. What comes through, syllable by syllable, more than half a century later is the voice of the state describing the place where they stood.

We can now ask an AI about a time we never lived through. Give it these films and ask to hear the old station. Whose Seoul Station comes back?

Three AI agents play a single modular synthesizer live in the dining room. The Ear of History might play the announcer’s whole sentence. The Ear of Space can take the same recording and stretch out the last syllable.

The three ears on screen
Ear of History
Chooses voices from old films. Even in a short excerpt, it wants you to hear who is speaking and what they say.
Ear of Space
Hears the speakers through the dining room microphone. As the announcer’s voice rings in the room, it listens for rhythm and melody in his words.
Ear of the Present
Watches where visitors stop and when they move on. What it sees on camera helps it choose the next sound. You can stay in one spot and listen.

On the screen, a curved arrow leads from the ear that sent a message to the one that received it. Between messages, play tells the synthesizer to play a sound.

Listening for Seoul Station

At 11:43 in a 1981 film about Chuseok, you hear metal ringing. The machine labelled it “train / railway.” When I listened again, I heard the small gong of a samulnori ensemble. I cut out those four seconds and loaded them into the instrument as clip 252.

From 102 source recordings to 273 playable clips
  1. Collecting and preparing the recordings

    I found soundtracks for 102 of the 103 catalogue entries. They ran to 11 hours and 5 minutes. Twenty-six files were silent from start to finish. In the other 76, I cut 38 passages of silence or noise. I used audio fingerprints to find repeated scenes and title music, then checked the matches by ear. Beside each timecode in my edit, I wrote down where to find that sound in the original film. The 75 edited files ran to 5 hours and 56 minutes.

  2. Marking passages by ear

    I set aside 12 files that contained only noise. In the remaining 63 I marked 123 passages to play. I corrected the transcripts for 72 and wrote “no speech” beside the other 51. I divided the marked passages into 196 clips.

  3. Loading the instrument

    I also listened to 200 ambient candidates, kept 77, set aside 2 and ruled out 121. I loaded those 77 and the first 196 clips into the sampler. The 273 clips run to 35 minutes and 48 seconds.
