The Reciting Machine

읊는 기계

Listen to the two recordingsMaking the archive

In the 1969 newsreel, Seoul Station is still in the future tense. It will serve only passenger trains, the announcer says. Trains moved to the new station in 2004. Play the recording in the old station’s upstairs Small Dining Room and he is still describing this building’s future.

Listening for the station behind that voice, I gathered newsreels and cultural films. Two thirds of the sound was narration. You can barely hear the people on the platform talking. Half a century later, the state’s account of where they stood is still clear down to the last syllable.

I loaded the recordings into a modular synthesizer played by three AI agents. On 22 November, one took a 1968 report on the Samgakji overpass and looped the end of “Samgakji” into a beat. Another brought the announcer back. The third played a pipe organ from a 1961 film.

The films are seven years apart. That day, one bar separated the voice and the organ. As we listen for a station we never knew, whose Seoul Station comes back?

Three AI agents play one modular synthesizer live in the dining room. The Ear of History might let a whole sentence play. The Ear of Space can take its last syllable and stretch it out.

The three ears on screen
Ear of History
Chooses voices from the films. Even in a short excerpt, it wants the speaker’s voice and the sense of the words to come through.
Ear of Space
Listens through the room microphone to the sound coming back from the speakers. In the announcer’s voice it listens for rhythm and melody.
Ear of the Present
Watches visitors on camera as they pause or move on. Their movements help it choose what to play next. You can stay in one spot and listen.

The three ears’ conversation appears on screen in large type. Curved arrows show which way each message went. Words like play are commands the ears send to the synthesizer.

Listening for Seoul Station

About 11 minutes and 43 seconds into a 1981 film about Chuseok, you can hear metal ringing. The machine labelled it “train / railway.” Listening again, I recognised a kkwaenggwari, the small gong in a samulnori ensemble. I cut out those four seconds and put them in slot 252 on the sampler.

From 102 source recordings to 273 playable clips
  1. Collecting and preparing the recordings

    I found soundtracks for 102 of the 103 films in the catalogue, totalling 11 hours and 5 minutes. Twenty-six files were silent from start to finish. From the other 76 I cut 38 stretches of silence or noise. Audio fingerprints helped me find repeated scenes and theme music. I listened to the matches before cutting the duplicates. I kept the original and edited timecodes side by side so I could find each sound again. That left 75 files and 5 hours and 56 minutes of sound.

  2. Marking passages by ear

    I set aside 12 files that contained only noise. In the remaining 63, I marked 123 passages for the instrument. I listened back to the 72 passages with speech and corrected their transcripts. Beside the other 51 I wrote “no speech.” I cut the marked passages into 196 clips.

  3. Loading the instrument

    I listened to another 200 excerpts for their ambient sounds. I kept 77, ruled out 121 and left 2 undecided. The 77 went into the sampler alongside the 196 clips I had already cut. The instrument holds 273 clips in all, totalling 35 minutes and 48 seconds.
