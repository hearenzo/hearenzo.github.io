The Reciting Machine

읊는 기계

Listen to the two recordingsMaking the archive

In a film from 1969, Seoul Station still exists in the future tense. The trains moved to the new station in 2004. Yet when the voice plays again upstairs in the old station’s Small Dining Room, Seoul Station is still about to become something.

I collected Korean Newsreels and cultural films to find out what the old station sounded like. Two thirds of the sound was narration. You can scarcely hear what people might have said to each other on the platform. What comes through, syllable by syllable, more than half a century later is the voice of the state describing the place where they stood.

We can now ask an AI about a time we never lived through. Give it these films and ask to hear the old station. Whose Seoul Station comes back?

Three AI agents play one modular synthesizer live in the dining room. One plays the announcer’s sentence to the end. Another draws out its last syllable.

The three ears on the screen
Ear of History
Searches the films for a voice. Even after making a cut, it wants you to hear who spoke and what they said.
Ear of Space
Listens to the sound in the dining room through a microphone. It can hold the last syllable long after the announcer has finished speaking.
Ear of the Present
Uses a camera to see where people linger and when they move on. It takes their movements into account when choosing what to play next. You can stand wherever you like and listen.

The gallery screen shows the agents’ messages to one another and the commands they send to the instrument.

Listening for Seoul Station

Eleven minutes and forty-three seconds into a 1981 film about Chuseok, metal rings for four seconds. The machine labelled it “train / railway.” I listened again and wrote “kkwaenggwari, a samulnori ensemble.” The small gong is now clip 252 in the instrument.

From 102 source recordings to 273 playable clips
  1. Collecting and preparing the recordings

    Of 103 catalogue entries, 102 yielded soundtracks, totalling 11 hours and 5 minutes. Twenty-six were silent throughout. From the 76 with a signal, I trimmed 38 passages containing only silence or noise. I used audio fingerprints to find repeated scenes and title music, then checked the matches by ear. I noted the original timecode alongside the edited one so I could find each passage again. The 75 edited files ran to 5 hours and 56 minutes.

  2. Marking passages by ear

    After setting aside 12 noise-only files, I marked 123 passages in the remaining 63. I corrected the transcripts of 72 and marked the other 51 as containing no speech. These passages yielded 196 playable clips.

  3. Loading the instrument

    I also listened to 200 ambient candidates, kept 77, set aside 2 and ruled out 121. Together with the first 196 clips, they form the modular sampler’s bank of 273 sounds, lasting 35 minutes and 48 seconds.
